## 1. Governing Physics: The Death of the Stagnant Layer

In a traditional straight-pipe heat exchanger, fluid flow eventually settles into a fully developed parabolic velocity profile governed by the Hagen-Poiseuille relationship. Near the conduit walls, the fluid velocity drops to zero—a phenomenon dictated by the fluid dynamic no-slip boundary condition. This forms a viscous and <TechnicalTangent term="Thermal Boundary Layer" category="Thermodynamics // Interface" definition="A stagnant or slow-moving fluid region adjacent to a solid wall where heat transfer is dominated by conduction rather than convection." equation="Nu = (h * L) / k" />: a slow-moving layer of fluid that acts as an insulating blanket. Because heat must conduct across this laminar sublayer before it can be carried away by bulk advection, convective heat transfer coefficients remain strictly capped.

Helical coils break this limitation by coupling axial momentum with centrifugal acceleration. As fluid travels through a curved path of radius $R$, the higher-velocity fluid in the core of the conduit experiences a greater centrifugal force than the slower fluid near the walls. This centrifugal imbalance pushes the core fluid toward the outer wall (the extrados). To satisfy the continuity equation, the displaced fluid near the outer boundary is forced along the circumference back toward the inner wall (the intrados). This sets up a pair of stable, counter-rotating secondary flow spirals known as <TechnicalTangent term="Dean Vortices" category="Fluid Dynamics // Kinematics" definition="Secondary counter-rotating flow vortices induced in curved conduits when centrifugal forces overcome viscous shear." equation="De = Re * sqrt(d / D)" />.

The intensity of this convective secondary motion is parameterized by the dimensionless Dean number ($De$):

$$De = Re \cdot \sqrt{\frac{d}{D}}$$

Where $Re$ is the axial Reynolds number, $d$ is the internal tube diameter, and $D$ is the coil curvature diameter. As $De$ exceeds 40, secondary circulation begins to actively scour the thermal boundary layer, elevating the convective heat transfer coefficient $h$ by up to 300% over an equivalent straight conduit.

## 2. Component Architecture: The Geometry of the Helical Bundle

A production helical heat exchanger is an engineered packaging assembly balancing coil curvature ratio ($d/D$), axial pitch ($p$), and shell manifolding. The primary functional members comprise:
1. **The Helical Coil Bundle:** Typically seamless cold-drawn 316L stainless steel or oxygen-free copper tubing.
2. **The Central Mandrel / Core Displacement Plug:** An internal cylinder that forces external shell fluid through the compact annular spaces between turns rather than bypassing down the center.
3. **The Shroud / Pressure Envelope:** A perimeter sleeve maintaining uniform fluid velocities across the outer coil surface.

The dimensionless curvature ratio $\delta = d/D$ dictates the transition threshold from laminar to turbulent flow. In straight pipes, turbulence triggers around $Re \approx 2,300$. In helical conduits, Dean vortices stabilize the laminar flow against random velocity fluctuations, postponing transition to significantly higher critical Reynolds numbers:

$$Re_{\text{crit}} = 2{,}100 \cdot \left(1 + 12 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{d}{D}}\right)$$

For a compact coil with $d = 8 \text{ mm}$ and $D = 40 \text{ mm}$ ($\delta = 0.20$), the flow remains laminar up to $Re_{\text{crit}} \approx 13{,}300$. Even while technically laminar, the vigorous Dean mixing provides heat transfer performance rivaling turbulent straight flow, but with significantly lower high-frequency pressure oscillations.

## 3. Manufacturing & DFM: CNC Mandrel Bending & Springback

Fabricating precision helical coils without tube buckling, wrinkling, or excessive wall thinning requires multi-axis CNC rotary draw bending with internal mandrel tooling. As the tube is swept around the bend die, the outer wall (extrados) is subjected to intense tensile strain, while the inner wall (intrados) experiences compressive stress.

Without internal hydraulic or mechanical support, the cross-section inevitably collapses into an ellipse. The permissible cross-sectional out-of-roundness (ovality) is strictly capped at $5\%$ to prevent localized flow choking and unpredictable pressure drops:

$$\text{Ovality (\%)} = \frac{d_{\text{max}} - d_{\text{min}}}{d_{\text{nominal}}} \times 100 \le 5.0\%$$

**C12200 Deoxidized High Residual Phosphorus (DHP) Copper** provides outstanding thermal conductivity ($k \approx 390 \text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$). However, its annealed yield strength is modest ($\sigma_y \approx 70 \text{ MPa}$), and it is vulnerable to boundary erosion when secondary fluid velocities exceed $2.5 \text{ m/s}$. The swirling Dean vortices continuously blast the copper surface with abrasive fluid shear, stripping the protective copper oxide passivation film ($\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$).

For demanding environments involving caustic fluids, superheated steam, or high mechanical pressures, **AISI 316L Austenitic Stainless Steel** is the standard. While its bulk conductivity is lower ($k \approx 16.3 \text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$), its elevated yield strength ($\sigma_y \ge 220 \text{ MPa}$) permits much thinner wall tubing ($0.5 \text{ mm}$ vs $1.2 \text{ mm}$ for copper). Because convective thermal resistance ($1/h$) dominates over conductive wall resistance ($t/k$), 316L achieves nearly identical overall heat transfer coefficients ($U$) while supporting operational pressures in excess of $150 \text{ Bar}$.

2. **Circumferential Thin-Film RTD Arrays:** Micro-machined resistance temperature detectors deposited directly onto the outer wall measure the temperature gradient at $15^\circ$ circumferential increments, empirically confirming that local heat flux peaks at the extrados ($\theta = 180^\circ$) where Dean impingement is most intense.

The overall pressure drop penalty is governed by the Ito correlation:

$$f_{\text{helical}} = f_{\text{straight}} \cdot \left[Re \cdot \left(\frac{d}{D}\right)^2\right]^{1/20} \quad \text{for } De > 30$$

While pumping power increases by $15\% - 35\%$, the $300\%$ increase in heat transfer density allows hardware architects to slash total exchanger packaging volume by over $60\%$, unlocking radical thermal density in space-constrained aerospace and compute hardware.
